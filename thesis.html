My master's thesis journey

I started the year by taking a course in ring theory, which gave me some of the algebraic background I was missing. This turned out to be surprisingly useful: rings are one of the basic ingredients of algebraic geometry, and learning some algebraic geometry was in turn essential for understanding toric geometry (a subject that became a major part of my thesis).

My thesis looked at topological string theory, a simplified version of string theory where many of the complicated physical questions become much more manageable mathematically. In particular, I focused on topological strings on a special class of spaces called toric Calabi–Yau threefolds.

A large part of the project was therefore learning how to describe these spaces geometrically. I used toric geometry to turn complicated geometric problems into something that could be studied using combinatorics and algebra. This eventually led to a particularly useful object called the topological vertex, which provides a way of calculating quantities in topological string theory by breaking a complicated geometry into simpler pieces.

I then used the topological vertex to calculate the partition function and free energy of a simple example, and compared the result with two other approaches: a description using Chern–Simons theory and a calculation coming from the B-model of topological strings. Seeing these apparently different methods give the same answer was one of the most interesting parts of the project.

The story becomes even more interesting when we look at what happens near certain special points in the geometry. This led me to the connection between topological strings and spectral theory, where geometric information can be encoded in quantum-mechanical operators. I studied how the so-called mirror curve of the geometry can be quantised, and looked at a particular spectral quantity. This spectral quantity relates to the free energy of the topological string and is what underlies the Topological String/Spectral Theory (TS/ST) correspondence.

Finally, I looked at a less well-understood example involving the geometry called local F1. This is the simplest geometry for which a spectral quantity of interest in the TS/ST correspondence is not yet known. Finding that is of great importance in verifying the correspondence.

In short: the project took me from ring theory and algebraic geometry, through toric geometry, and eventually into topological strings, mirror symmetry and spectral theory. It taught me plenty of abstract mathematics useful in theoretical physics and above all motivated me to learn more.
